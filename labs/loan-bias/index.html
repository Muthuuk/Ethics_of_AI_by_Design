This lab follows Chapter 4, Exercise 3: build a simplified loan approval system, introduce bias into its training data, show the effects, then apply a fairness-aware adjustment. Everyone and every number here is invented for learning. In this pretend world, people in Group A and Group B are equally able to repay. Any difference in how they are treated comes from the data.

Your tasks

  1. Start fair: 50% share, no past unfairness. Note that both groups are treated alike.
  2. Raise unfairness in past decisions. Watch Group B approvals fall. Nobody told the model to favour Group A: it copied the old pattern.
  3. Now lower the share of Group B to 10%. Watch the red rings grow in Group B. With less data about them, the model makes more mistakes for them.
  4. Turn on the fairness-aware adjustment. Which problem does it fix? Which one is still left?
  5. Finally, raise the share back to 50%. What fixes the remaining problem?

What this shows

Two separate problems come from the data. Past unfairness teaches the model to repeat an old gap in approvals. Unrepresentative data makes the model less accurate for the group it saw less of, as the Gender Shades study found for face-analysis tools.

A fairness-aware adjustment can close the approval gap. But it cannot create knowledge the data never had: to fix the accuracy gap you need more representative data. That is why the course says to check who is in the data, and who is missing, before training.

This is a deliberately simple teaching model, not a real credit system.
